Mapping the Pandemic Landscape: Final Report

Evan Kennedy elk2149, Amrutha Banda ab6169, Kaveri Uberoy kiu2103, Suhina Sharma ss7636

Motivation

The overarching goal of this project was to understand how structural, demographic, socioeconomic, political, and behavioral factors shaped COVID-19 outcomes across U.S. counties from 2020 to 2023. While the virus spreads biologically, the pandemic’s impacts were profoundly influenced by entrenched social determinants of health. Our project therefore examined how variables such as race, poverty, income inequality, mask usage, political affiliation, and community violence related to county-level COVID-19 cases, deaths, and severity.

We approached the problem through three major components. The first was the development of an interactive Shiny map designed to visualize spatial disparities and compare outcomes across groups such as high- and low-poverty counties or counties with high and low proportions of Black residents. The second involved statistical analyses examining the relationships between poverty, race, inequality, and COVID-19 mortality over time. The third explored how violence - including homicide rates, violent crime, and fatal police shootings - related to COVID-19 outcomes. Together, these components aimed to reveal how structural inequities shaped the trajectory of the pandemic.

Related Work

In particular, we were inspired by research highlighting racial disparities in Covid-19 outcomes, including “Hospitalization and Mortality among Black Patients and White Patients with Covid-19”, Price-Haywood et al. (2020) and “Racial Health Disparities and Covid-19 - Caution and Context”, Chowkwanyun & Reed (2020), which we learned about in our introduction to public health course, which explore how systemic factors like healthcare access and ‘weathering’ impact disease severity among Black Americans. These papers highlighted how, due to systemic biases, black people are more likely to have their health concerns dismissed or not sufficiently addressed as compared to white people, and do not receive the same level of care overall, and therefore are more likely to experience severe cases of Covid. While we were initially interested in racial disparities, we expanded our inquiry to include additional county-level factors such as poverty, political affiliation, and mask use. We also wanted to explore whether county-level violence indicators were related to COVID-19 outcomes in the United States. Early in the pandemic, several national reports described rising community violence and highlighted how structural conditions influenced health risks. Motivated by these patterns, our question asked whether places with higher levels of violence also experienced worse COVID-19 outcomes. In class exercises like mapping datasets such as Airbnb listings and exploring Shiny apps inspired our approach to interactive county-level visualization. We used public datasets from The New York Times (COVID-19 cases and deaths) and a public dataset that compiled Census/ACS demographic data, which allowed us to link health outcomes with socioeconomic and political variables. Our work aims to combine public health, demographic, and political data into a unified, interactive visualization framework.

Initial Questions

At the outset, we aimed to answer several broad questions: How did COVID-19 cases and deaths vary geographically across U.S. counties? Were there identifiable patterns associated with racial composition, poverty levels, political affiliation, or mask usage? Could these patterns be meaningfully visualized interactively? As we expanded the project, additional questions emerged. For the Shiny map, we asked how temporal trends interacted with structural factors across multiple years and whether counties with higher proportions of Black residents, higher poverty, or lower mask use experienced disproportionate severity. For the poverty and race statistical analyses, we focused on how inequality, poverty, and racial composition predicted COVID-19 outcomes and how these relationships changed over time. For the violence analysis, our original interest in fatal police shootings evolved once we saw how sparse the data were, leading us to investigate homicide rates and violent crime as potentially more informative indicators of community vulnerability.

Data

Shiny Map Data

The Shiny map relied on six datasets. COVID-19 data came from the New York Times daily county-level data from 2020 through 2023. Mask usage data came from a July 2020 nationwide survey. Demographic, socioeconomic, and political data were taken from a compiled ACS/Census dataset including race, income, and poverty variables. Data cleaning required harmonizing county names and FIPS codes across datasets, calculating cumulative and yearly COVID-19 cases and deaths, computing per-100,000 rates, merging demographic and political data with COVID-19 outcomes, and preparing mask usage variables. We initially struggled because some ‘counties’ in the demographic dataset we had were not considered counties by the NYT data, such as census areas in Alaska and boroughs of NYC. Another issue we came across was that we could not cleanly add up all the covid data, because it was already cumulative in some senses (so we were getting huge, unrealistic numbers when we cumulated across the data), so we needed to adjust how we dealt with these different situations by cumulating differently and choosing how we wanted to include each county. We defined high-exposure groups using 75th quantiles for racial proportion and mask use, and a 15% cutoff for poverty (although 20% is how it is defined by the USDA, to give us a more extensive, clean visual).

Poverty and Race Analysis Data

The poverty and race dataset used the dataset with compiled county-level COVID-19 cases and deaths with demographic and socioeconomic measures per county. COVID-19 outcomes were measured as cases and deaths per capita to ensure comparability across counties of different sizes. To calculate these per-capita measures, the number of cases and deaths for each year was divided by the county’s population. Because only one population estimate was available per county, we assumed population size remained relatively stable across the four-year period. The key socioeconomic variables used in this analysis were the poverty rate and an income inequality index defined as the ratio of the 80th percentile income to the 20th percentile income (P80/P20). This index reflects the degree of income disparity within each county, with higher values indicating a larger gap between high- and low-income households. Racial composition was represented as the proportions of Black, White, Asian, and Hispanic residents, standardized as percentages of county population.

Violence Analysis Data

For the violence analysis, we merged county-level COVID-19 cases and deaths from 2020 with homicide rates, violent crime rates, and fatal police shooting data. Fatal police shootings were reshaped from wide to long format, summed across 2017–2020, and converted to per-100,000 population. Homicide and violent crime rates were also standardized per-capita. The final dataset included one row per county representing all COVID-19 and violence measures.

Exploratory Analysis

Shiny Map Exploratory Analysis

To explore geographic patterns, we built an interactive Shiny map that allows users to compare the spread of covid in high versus low exposure groups for variables such as poverty, racial composition, mask usage, and political affiliation. Users can toggle years (2020–2023) and metrics (cases, deaths, and death-to-case ratio, which we treated as a severity measure), which define the color spread. We used side-by-side maps to allow direct comparison across groups. Because initial color gradients hid meaningful variation, we used a logarithmic transformation for visualization. We also added the severity metric to highlight disparities that raw case counts obscured. The map initially loaded very slowly due to the complexity of mapping all 3000 U.S. counties as polygons.

Poverty and Race Exploratory Analysis

For socioeconomic and racial variables, we constructed scatterplots showing relationships between inequality, poverty, and COVID-19 death and case rates. Clear positive associations emerged between inequality and COVID-19 deaths, and between poverty and COVID outcomes. Temporal breakdowns revealed that inequality had a smaller relationship with cases early in the pandemic but a strong relationship with deaths in 2020–2021. This could be because structural vulnerabilities that increase mortality risk, such as limited access to treatment and the lack of vaccines, lead to greater fatality. These factors made infections far more likely to result in death early in the pandemic, even before many cases were fully detected or recorded. Inequality became a stronger predictor of case rates beginning in 2022, a trend likely driven by more uniform virus spread and improved testing showed clearer disparities in exposure and infection rates. We also created interactive heatmaps to examine racial disparities. Counties with larger Black and Hispanic populations showed strong positive associations with death rates in 2020 and 2021, while Asian population proportion showed a strong negative association with deaths across all years.

Violence Exploratory Analysis

We began by visualizing the 15 counties with the highest fatal police shooting rates between 2017 and 2020. A horizontal bar chart with a color scale for COVID-19 deaths revealed that although a few counties had very high fatal shooting rates, their COVID-19 death rates varied widely. This suggested that fatal police shootings alone might not be a reliable indicator of community vulnerability during the pandemic. We then created a scatterplot of fatal police shootings per 100,000 residents against COVID-19 deaths per 100,000. Most counties had zero or very few fatal shootings, and the regression line was nearly flat. This confirmed that fatal police shootings showed little meaningful relationship with COVID-19 mortality. Next, we explored violent crime rates, which were more continuous and varied across counties. A scatterplot revealed a positive trend, with higher violent crime rates associated with higher COVID-19 death rates. Although the relationship was noisy, the upward slope suggested that broader patterns of community violence might be linked to higher COVID-19 mortality. These early visualizations guided our decision to continue the analysis using formal regression models that examined each violence measure both separately and together.

Additional Analysis

Shiny Map Additional Analysis

There were some initial issues with the map, such as the way the heat map colors the spread of covid, since many counties were pretty similar when viewed in the lens of larger scales. We ended up using a logarithmic transformation to make the spread differences more dramatic/ visible when it came to the heat map. Also, the covid severity metric was an important addition because the covid cases and deaths on their own were pretty variable and widespread, but once we mapped by deaths per case, then it was a lot more apparent how different socioeconomic exposures impacted covid severity. We decided against using per capita values for cases and deaths, so the map could also show how case and death counts were concentrated around city areas/ generally spread geographically, but the covid severity metric acted as a useful way to standardize severity to view the impact of covid on each county. Also, we used the mapshaper tool to speed up loading the map.

Poverty and Race Statistical Analysis

We fitted interaction regression models with inequality × year interactions for both COVID-19 cases and deaths. Inequality had minimal impact on cases in 2020–2021 but became strongly predictive in 2022, then lost significance in 2023 as overall cases declined. Inequality was a strong predictor of deaths in 2020 and 2021, with diminishing effects as vaccines and treatments became widespread.

Racial composition analyses showed that Asian population proportion consistently predicted decreased mortality. This could be associated with factors such as better healthcare access, higher vaccination rates, and strong community adherence to preventive measures. However, it is important to note that the overall representation of the Asian population in many counties is relatively low, which may amplify the perceived significance of this relationship despite a limited sample size. Black population proportion was positively associated with mortality in 2020 and 2021, reflecting structural disadvantages such as uninsured rates, overrepresentation in essential work, and crowded housing that increased exposure risk early in the pandemic. Interestingly, Hispanic population proportion was significant in 2020, 2022, and 2023, reflecting ongoing disparities in exposure and vaccine access; these patterns may appear weaker in scatterplots but were detected in the statistical models. This may be because factors like varying local policies, differences in vaccine rollout timing, or other unmeasured socioeconomic variables may create complex or non-linear patterns that weaken visible trends in scatterplots but are still detected by the statistical models.

White population proportion showed negative correlations early in the pandemic but flattened over time and also showed significant associations in 2020, 2022, and 2023. This may be due to a combination of factors such as differences in population distribution, better healthcare access, and vaccine access in more wealthy areas, and lower overall exposure in certain regions.

Future analyses will examine race × poverty interactions to better understand combined structural effects.

Violence Statistical Analysis

We began our formal analysis with a crude linear regression modeling COVID-19 deaths as a function of fatal police shootings per 100,000. This model showed almost no association, which matched the exploratory plots. In the second model, we examined violent crime rates and found a small but clear positive relationship. Counties with higher violent crime rates tended to experience more COVID-19 deaths, although the effect size was modest.

The third and most informative model included all three violence indicators at once: fatal police shootings, homicide rates, and violent crime rates. This allowed us to isolate the independent contribution of each factor. We then used the model to create marginal effects plots that predicted COVID-19 deaths while holding the other predictors at their median values. These plots showed that homicide rates had the strongest and steepest upward association with COVID-19 deaths. Violent crime rates also displayed a positive relationship, although the effect was smaller. Fatal police shootings remained essentially flat, suggesting that once general violence levels were accounted for, fatal police shootings did not meaningfully predict COVID-19 mortality.

Discussion

Shiny Map Findings

Especially in 2020, COVID-19 cases and deaths were concentrated around major metropolitan areas- particularly in the Northeast, Midwest, and parts of the South- reflecting population density. However, nearly all counties experienced significant burdens by 2021. The severity metric revealed the most meaningful disparities. Counties with high proportions of Black residents and counties with high poverty levels showed substantially higher deaths-per-case in 2020 and 2021. These patterns suggest that socioeconomic disadvantage was associated with more severe outcomes even when total case counts were comparable, supporting prior research indicating that structural factors such as differential access to healthcare, occupational exposure, and chronic stress weathering contributed to disproportionate pandemic impacts in Black communities. Mask usage did not show clear differences, likely due to limitations of the July 2020 single-timepoint dataset. Political affiliation also did not show strong or consistent differences in Covid severity. Early in the pandemic, Democratic leaning counties appeared darker on the case and death maps, but this seems primarily driven by the fact that major cities where early outbreaks were concentrated tend to vote Democratic. Once case and death counts later spread more evenly across the country, these differences largely diminished, and the political majority had no obvious relationship with severity in our visualization. Overall, severity revealed structural inequities masked by raw counts.

Poverty and Race Findings

Inequality and poverty were strongly associated with COVID-19 deaths, particularly in the early years when vaccines and treatments were not yet widely available. The relationship between inequality and case rates strengthened in 2022. Racial disparities followed temporal patterns: Black and Hispanic populations were strongly associated with elevated mortality early in the pandemic, while Asian populations consistently showed reduced mortality. These findings reflect structural inequities in healthcare access, occupational risk, and living conditions. The dynamics of racial associations across years suggest evolving influences of vaccines, variant waves, and policy landscapes.

Violence Findings

Homicide rates and violent crime rates were meaningfully associated with COVID-19 mortality, whereas fatal police shootings were not. Because fatal shootings are rare events, they fail to capture broader structural vulnerabilities associated with community violence. In contrast, homicide and violent crime rates reflect chronic stress, economic instability, and systemic disadvantage, all of which likely exacerbated susceptibility to severe COVID-19 outcomes. These findings highlight how the pandemic magnified existing structural harms rather than affecting communities uniformly.

Conclusion

By integrating spatial visualization, socioeconomic and racial analyses, and community violence measures, this project demonstrates that COVID-19 outcomes were shaped by deep structural inequalities across the United States. Severity - not raw case counts - revealed the most striking disparities. Poverty, inequality, racial composition, and community violence all contributed to patterns of vulnerability, especially in the early years of the pandemic. These results reinforce longstanding evidence that structural determinants of health shape both everyday wellbeing and crisis outcomes, and they point toward the need for policy responses that address underlying inequities rather than only acute health threats.
