Shiny Map Analysis

Motivation, Related Works, and Initial Questions.

The Covid-19 pandemic affected communities across the United States in highly uneven ways. While the virus itself spreads biologically, the outcomes in terms of cases and deaths were strongly shaped by social, economic, and structural factors. This map aims to investigate these disparities by allowing us to visualize county-level Covid-19 cases and deaths alongside key contextual variables such as race, poverty, political affiliation, and mask usage. By visualizing and analyzing these relationships, we hoped to gain insight into the social determinants of health that influenced pandemic outcomes, and to understand how they might have contributed to the severity of the pandemic in different regions.

In particular, we were inspired by research highlighting racial disparities in Covid-19 outcomes, including “Hospitalization and Mortality among Black Patients and White Patients with Covid-19”, Price-Haywood et al. (2020) and “Racial Health Disparities and Covid-19 - Caution and Context”, Chowkwanyun & Reed (2020), which we learned about in our introduction to public health course, which explore how systemic factors like healthcare access and ‘weathering’ impact disease severity among Black Americans. These papers highlighted how, due to systemic biases, black people are more likely to have their health concerns dismissed or not sufficiently addressed as compared to white people, and do not receive the same level of care overall, and therefore are more likely to experience severe cases of Covid. While we were initially interested in racial disparities, we expanded our inquiry to include additional county-level factors such as poverty, political affiliation, and mask use. In class exercises like mapping datasets such as Airbnb listings and exploring Shiny apps inspired our approach to interactive county-level visualization. For the map, we used public datasets from The New York Times (COVID-19 cases and deaths) and a public dataset that compiled Census/ACS demographic data, which allowed us to link health outcomes with socioeconomic and political variables. Our work aims to combine public health, demographic, and political data into a unified, interactive visualization framework.

We began the project seeking to answer : how did Covid-19 cases and deaths vary geographically at the county level across the U.S.? Were there patterns related to racial composition, poverty, political affiliation, or mask usage? Could we visualize these patterns in an interactive, comparative format? As our analysis progressed, additional questions emerged: How did temporal trends in Covid-19 outcomes interact with social and political factors across multiple years? Were counties with higher proportions of Black residents, higher poverty, or different political affiliations disproportionately affected in terms of cases or deaths? How did mask usage correlate with disease spread in the context of other county-level variables?

Data: selection and cleaning.

Our data came mainly from 6 datasets for the shiny map: COVID-19 data, one data set for each year from the New York Times county-level datasets (2020–2023) with per day cases and deaths, as well as a mask use dataset which came from survey data from July 2020. Demographic, socioeconomic, and political data data came from one public dataset which compiled information from ACS and census sources, including race, income, and poverty.

Data cleaning involved harmonizing county names and FIPS codes across datasets, calculating cumulative and yearly Covid-19 cases and deaths, computing per-100,000 population rates, merging demographic, political, and health data with COVID-19 outcomes, and preparing mask usage and other behavioral variables for comparison. We initially struggled because some ‘counties’ in the demographic dataset we had were not considered counties by the NYT data, such as census areas in Alaska and boroughs of NYC. Another issue we came across was that we could not cleanly add up all the covid data, because it was already cumulative in some senses, so we needed to adjust how we dealt with these different situations. We set some boundaries semi-arbitrarily for comparing different high factor and low factor groups; for race, we selected the populations where the proportion of black population was above the 75th quantile across all counties as ‘High proportion of black population’, as for masking as well, but for poverty, we used 15% as the cut off for high poverty rate, although 20% is how it is defined by the USDA, to give us a more extensive, clean visual.

Exploratory Analysis

We made the interactive shiny map to allow users to: compare two groups (e.g., high vs low poverty, Republican vs Democrat counties, high vs low mask usage), select Covid-19 metric (cases , deaths, or covid ratio, ie deaths per cases, an indicator of severity) and year (2020–2023) to visualize case or death counts per county and explore demographic and political covariates directly via pop-ups and filtering on the map. We wanted this map to be able to look at the geographic spread of cases and deaths linked with various different factors, to view alongside our statistical analyses of poverty and race to Covid-19, and also to look at other factors that might be relevant to disease spread and severity, such as mask usage and political majority per county. We decided to show two maps side by side, so it was easier to see the differences in spread depending on the exposure of choice (a high and low poverty map, high and low black proportion of the population, republican vs democrat, high vs low mask usage).

Additional analysis

There were some initial issues with the map, such as the way the heat map colors the spread of covid, since many counties were pretty similar when viewed in the lens of larger scales. We ended up using a logarithmic transformation to make the spread differences more dramatic/ visible when it came to the heat map. Also, the covid severity metric was an important addition because the covid cases and deaths on their own were pretty variable and widespread, but once we mapped by deaths per case, then it was a lot more apparent how different socioeconomic exposures impacted covid severity. We decided against using per capita values for cases and deaths, so the map could also show how case and death counts were concentrated around city areas/ generally spread geographically, but the covid severity metric acted as a useful way to standardize severity to view the impact of covid on each county. Also, our page was initially extremely slow and crashed when trying to launch the app on a browser because we had to map each county as a polygon onto the map, which involved generating over 3000 polygons and a pop up for each county, so we used the mapshaper tool to speed it up, which helped a lot.

Findings from the map

Across all four years of available data 2020 2023, several clear geographic patterns emerged from the interactive Shiny map. Covid 19 cases and deaths were heavily concentrated in and around major metropolitan areas, particularly during the first two years of the pandemic. Counties containing or adjacent to large cities especially in the Northeast, Midwest, and parts of the South displayed the highest counts. This pattern largely reflects population density: areas with more residents simply had more opportunities for transmission, and therefore accumulated more reported cases and deaths. At the same time, Covid 19 was geographically widespread, and most counties across the United States rural and urban alike ultimately experienced substantial case and death burdens by the end of 2021, illustrating how the virus reached every part of the country regardless of county level demographic or political characteristics.

However, the most meaningful differences emerged not from the raw case or death counts but from the Covid severity metric deaths per case. Mapping severity revealed disparities that were less visible in simple counts. Counties with a higher proportion of Black residents consistently showed darker severity shading, particularly in 2020 and 2021, indicating higher death to case ratios. This pattern parallels prior research showing that structural factors such as differential access to healthcare, occupational exposure, and chronic stress weathering contributed to disproportionate pandemic impacts in Black communities. A similar trend appeared when comparing counties by poverty status: high poverty counties exhibited substantially higher Covid severity than low poverty counties during the early pandemic. These patterns suggest that socioeconomic disadvantage was associated with more severe outcomes even when total case counts were comparable.

In contrast, mask usage did not demonstrate the clear gradient we expected. The high mask usage and low mask usage maps looked relatively similar in both cases and severity. This likely reflects limitations of the dataset: mask use was measured only once, in July 2020, early in the pandemic and before major surges later that year and in 2021. Because it was based on self reported survey data, it may also reflect reporting biases or short term behavior rather than sustained prevention practices.

Political affiliation also did not show strong or consistent differences in Covid severity. Early in the pandemic, Democratic leaning counties appeared darker on the case and death maps, but this seems primarily driven by the fact that major cities where early outbreaks were concentrated tend to vote Democratic. Once case and death counts later spread more evenly across the country, these differences largely diminished, and the political majority had no obvious relationship with severity in our visualization.

Overall, the main conclusions from the map are that Covid 19 burden was widespread, but severity, not raw case counts revealed clearer social and structural disparities. Counties with higher proportions of Black residents and counties with high poverty levels experienced noticeably more severe outcomes, especially in 2020 and 2021. These findings are consistent with expectations from public health research, which has documented how structural inequities shape vulnerability to disease. The interactive map helps make these patterns visually accessible, enabling users to explore how demographic, socioeconomic, and political factors intersected with the trajectory of Covid 19 across the United States.
